ACTIVITY 2 · PROJECT HOUR

From purposes to learning outcomes

Bring your field research and tonight’s dialogue into your preliminary team. Together, decide what your DeCal could help people learn and draft three learning outcomes.

What you need to know

A learning outcome describes what a learner should be able to do after a learning experience. You’ve probably seen these in syllabi. Now you get to make the decisions underneath them: what is worth learning, who this is for, and why it matters.

Start with an action someone could demonstrate: explain, compare, interpret, design, or justify. Then name what they’ll work with and what a thoughtful response would involve. “Understand access” leaves a lot for the reader to guess. What would someone who understands access be able to explain or investigate?

For example: a DeCal about work and opportunity

An activity
“Discuss internships.”
This tells us what happens during class.

A learning outcome
“Compare two internship opportunities using information about pay, hours, and eligibility, and explain how those conditions affect who can participate.”
This tells us what learners should become able to do.

That choice already carries a purpose. Are we helping someone secure a position, examine who can afford to participate, or both? Marx and Labaree help us see why those intentions belong in the design conversation.

Work backward from the outcome

Backward design starts with the learning you want to make possible. Next, decide what learners could produce or demonstrate that would show it. Then choose the content and experiences that prepare them: what they need to read, watch, or learn about, and what they need to try, discuss, or make.

That includes preparation before class. In this course, the readings and your Pre-Work field research give you ideas and examples to think with. Dialogue and Project Hour give you opportunities to use them. When you design your DeCal, connect what learners encounter with what you ask them to do.

Follow the internship example

1. Outcome: compare opportunities and explain access. Imagine one posting offers an unpaid, 20-hour internship; another offers a paid, 8-hour placement. Both have eligibility requirements. Learners need to examine what those terms mean for someone’s actual circumstances.

2. Evidence: a comparison with reasons. Learners annotate the two postings and explain the tradeoffs for a student who also needs paid work. You can see whether they use the details to support their interpretation.

3. Content & experiences: prepare, then practice. Before class, learners could read a short text about access to work and a guide to the terms used in internship postings. In class, read one posting together, identify pay, hours, and eligibility, and use the text’s ideas to consider who could participate. Then compare a second posting in pairs. Finally, learners try a new comparison and explain their reasoning. The reading, guide, and postings supply the ideas and information they use in the activity.

That sequence uses scaffolding: giving learners the early knowledge, practice, and support they need to take on more complex work. This connects to Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development: the space between what learners can do independently and what they can do with guidance or a more capable peer. We’ll revisit this concept later in the course.

Tonight, you’ll sketch a first step toward one outcome. We’ll develop the course sequence in later weeks.
